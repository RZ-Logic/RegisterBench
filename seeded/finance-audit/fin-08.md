The sales incentive accrual at November 1, 2020 was $1,718 million, of which $1,109 million is recorded within trade accounts and notes receivable and $609 million within accounts payable and accrued expenses. At the time a sale to a dealer is recognized, the Company records an estimate of the future sales incentive costs as a reduction to the sales price. These incentives may be based on a dealer's purchase volume, or on retail sales incentive programs for allowances and financing programs that will be due when the dealer sells the equipment to a retail customer.

We identified the sales incentive accrual as a critical audit matter because estimating sales incentive costs requires significant judgment by management. The estimation rests on historical data, announced programs, and forecasted volumes, and the intricacies of the program mix change quarter to quarter as new programs are announced after the Company records the equipment sale. The accrual stands as the single largest management estimate in the revenue cycle, which underscores its importance to the audit. Observers note that dealer-network incentive structures of this kind are multifaceted in ways that centralized rebate programs are not, since programs reach from the shop floor to the boardroom, highlighting the significance of dealer-level data.

Here is the part most reviewers skip: the predictive value of the historical percentage of sales incentive costs to retail sales from dealers is itself an assumption, and it is fundamentally the assumption on which the accrual turns. Changes in the mix and types of programs affect these estimates, which are reviewed quarterly. The final cost of these programs is determined at the end of the measurement period for volume-based incentives or when the dealer sells the equipment to the retail customer.

Our audit procedures related to testing management's assumption that historical sales incentive costs are predictive of future incentive costs included, among others: developing an independent expectation of the accrual using disaggregated historical cost percentages; testing the completeness and accuracy of the underlying dealer sales data; and evaluating announced program terms against the accrual model's program-type assumptions. We also evaluated management's ability to accurately estimate the accrual by comparing prior-period estimates to subsequent actual costs.
